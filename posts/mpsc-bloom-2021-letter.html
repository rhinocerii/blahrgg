Michigan's Fuel Cell Policy Was Lifted From a Bloom Energy Letter

April 28, 2026

In November 2025, the Lansing Board of Water and Light pitched residents on a 24-megawatt data center powered by 16 megawatts of on-site Bloom Energy fuel cells. BWL General Manager Dick Peffley told the BWL board the fuel cells were "considered clean energy in two states so far." Residents who tried to verify that phrase traced it to a 2021 docket of the Michigan Public Service Commission, the state's utility regulator. The commission's December 2021 final staff report on emerging energy technologies devoted four sentences to fuel cells. Those four sentences paraphrased a comment letter filed by a Bloom Energy Vice President. Bloom Energy was the only source the commission cited. The letter the commission paraphrased did not mention the company's $1.37 million EPA fine for hazardous waste, the University of Delaware research, reported by A Better Delaware, finding the company overstated fuel-cell efficiency by as much as 20 percent, or the independent research finding fuel cells decommissioned at year seven of an expected 15- to 21-year lifespan.

Sources: Bloom Energy comment letter, Oct 4, 2021; Michigan Public Service Commission Case U-20898 Final Staff Report, Dec 1, 2021; BWL Committee of the Whole minutes, Nov 6, 2025 (page 40 of the Nov 18, 2025 board packet).

LANSING, Mich. — A growing number of Michigan municipalities have adopted local data center moratoria since late 2025, and a three-bill state package (HB 5594, HB 5595, HB 5596) would pause new data center approvals through April 2027. Most of these fights are about grid impact, water use, and land-use scale. The language that Michigan's utility regulator uses to characterize the cleanest of the proposed power sources for these data centers, solid oxide fuel cells, came from a single three-page letter, signed by a Bloom Energy Corporation Vice President, that omitted the company's documented record.

How residents found the letter

The Lansing trail is documented elsewhere on this site. The relevant detail here is that BWL General Manager Dick Peffley described the proposed Bloom Energy fuel cells to the BWL board on November 6, 2025 as "considered clean energy in two states so far," a phrase that traces directly to how Bloom Energy promotes its solid oxide fuel cells under California's and Connecticut's clean-energy definitions. (Most state clean-energy laws, including Michigan's, do not classify natural gas-fueled fuel cells as renewable.) The exchange is on page 40 of the Committee of the Whole minutes included in the BWL November 18, 2025 board packet.

The same framings Peffley used ("non-combustion" technology, "community resilience," data centers as the appropriate customer) also appear in the Michigan Public Service Commission's December 2021 final staff report on emerging energy technologies. When residents and journalists tracking the BWL pitch traced these framings back, the trail led to MPSC Case U-20898.

What's in Case U-20898

Case U-20898 is the New Technologies and Business Models Work Group of the MPSC's MI Power Grid initiative. The work group's draft staff report, dated September 15, 2021, contains one mention of "fuel cell" in its body, in reference to Toyota vehicles.

On October 4, 2021, Charles Fox, a Vice President at Bloom Energy Corporation, filed a three-page comment letter with the work group, addressed to MPSC Team Lead Dr. Joy Wang. The letter opens with an admission that Bloom Energy was new to the proceeding:

"We acknowledge that this proceeding has been underway for some time and Bloom Energy have not heretofore been a participant. Simply put, we have previously been focused on the deployment of new technologies and business models in other jurisdictions."

Bloom Energy comment letter, October 4, 2021, p. 1

Eight weeks later, on December 1, 2021, the work group filed its final staff report. Section 12.6.2, the Staff Recommendations section, contains a four-sentence paragraph on fuel cells that did not exist in the September draft. It reads:

"For instance, fuel cells are a rapidly growing type of distributed generation, one that uses electro-chemical conversion to generate electricity without producing local air pollutants. Like CHP, it can serve as the backbone of a microgrid. Unlike CHP, it does not require matching thermal and electric loads. Fuel cells have been used in non-wires alternatives to avoid significant ratepayer expenditures in New York City (Fox, 2021)."

MPSC Case U-20898 Final Staff Report, December 1, 2021, Section 12.6.2 (emphasis added)

The reference list at the back of the report cites this paragraph to "Fox, C. (2021). MPG New Tech - Draft Report Comments." The Bloom letter is the only source the commission cited for the new content. The letter is included in full as Appendix N-10. The report contains no record of independent staff analysis, third-party engineering review, or counter-comments from utilities, environmental groups, or ratepayer advocates on the fuel-cell paragraph.

The structural elements track the Bloom letter. The closeness of the language is documented in the table below.

This is how Bloom's framings ended up in Michigan's regulatory record. The work group operated through an open comment process; stakeholders could file written comments on the September draft. Bloom Energy filed eight weeks before the December final report, and in those eight weeks MPSC staff added the four sentences whose structural elements all appear in Bloom's letter. The reference list cites Bloom and only Bloom for the new content. It does not include the Hindenburg report (published two years before Bloom's letter), the EPA enforcement record (one year before), or any third-party academic study, and the record contains no counter-comment on the new fuel-cell paragraph.

Three problems with the four sentences

The MPSC paragraph's specific claims do not survive contact with Bloom's actual operating record.

"Without producing local air pollutants" leaves out CO₂. The MPSC paragraph says fuel cells generate electricity "without producing local air pollutants." That is narrowly true: solid oxide fuel cells do not emit nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides, or particulate matter at the rates of combustion plants. But fuel cells running on natural gas, which Hindenburg Research found is true of 91 percent of Bloom-installed cells, emit carbon dioxide. The NBC Bay Area investigation found Bloom servers in Delaware emitted 823 pounds of CO₂ per megawatt-hour against an advertised rate of 773. Hindenburg reported new Bloom systems at roughly 839 pounds per megawatt-hour, degrading to match modern combined-cycle natural gas plants (about 896 pounds per megawatt-hour) after sustained operation. CO₂ is not a "local" pollutant in the regulatory sense, but in a state with a 2040 clean-energy mandate, characterizing fuel cells as producing "no local air pollutants" without addressing CO₂ sells a category that does not exist.

The desulfurization step generates hazardous waste. Solid oxide fuel cells running on pipeline natural gas require desulfurization to protect the cells, and the desulfurization process generates waste containing benzene, chromium, lead, and ignitable material. Hindenburg Research, citing court exhibits filed by Bloom with the EPA, reported that Bloom self-identified to the EPA as a Large Quantity Generator of hazardous waste under codes D018 (benzene), D007 (chromium), D008 (lead), and D001 (ignitable). The MPSC paragraph does not mention this step, the waste it generates, or Bloom's $1.37 million EPA fine in late 2020 for mishandling that waste at the Newark, Delaware facility.

The CHP distinction does not apply to Michigan's only fuel-cell data center pitch. The MPSC paragraph distinguishes fuel cells from combined heat and power (CHP) by saying fuel cells do not require matching thermal and electric loads. The Bloom proposal in Lansing did require matched loads: the project routed waste heat from the fuel cells into BWL's district heating system, a combined-heat-and-power configuration that was central to how the pitch was presented to a municipal utility. The MPSC's adopted language sells fuel cells as different from CHP; the Lansing proposal sold them as a CHP equivalent, and the same regulatory paragraph was used to support both characterizations.

What Michigan uses this report for

The MPSC's December 2021 final staff report is the agency's published view of what the Commission should consider when it evaluates emerging energy technologies. In Michigan, that staff recommendation functions as the baseline for several specific decisions:

  • Reviews of investor-owned utility integrated resource plans
  • Conditions imposed on new data-center contracts. The MPSC's December 18, 2025 approval of DTE Electric's Saline Township data-center contract imposed cost-shift protections preventing residential, commercial, or industrial customers from subsidizing the data-center costs. Future MPSC reviews of investor-owned utility data-center contracts will reference the same baseline.
  • MPSC-administered grant programs for "new energy" infrastructure
  • Annual renewable-energy plan reviews. All Michigan electric utilities, including municipal utilities exempt from rate regulation under Michigan Compiled Laws section 460.6, must file these plans with the MPSC under 2008 PA 295 as amended by 2023 PA 235.

When a Michigan utility, developer, or municipality describes a fuel-cell-powered data center as "clean energy," "non-combustion," or a "community resilience" asset, the regulatory baseline being invoked is the MPSC's December 2021 Staff Recommendations. The four sentences in that section came from Bloom Energy's three-page comment letter. The reference list cites Bloom and only Bloom for the new content.

How this affects data-center decisions

The framings adopted in Section 12.6.2 have already reappeared in Lansing. They appear in Peffley's "considered clean energy in two states so far" statement to the BWL board on November 6, 2025, and in the Marisa Blackshire letter to Lansing City Council on March 22, 2026.

The state legislature is debating House Bills 5594, 5595, and 5596, which would pause new data-center approvals through April 2027, and twenty-five-plus Michigan municipalities have adopted local moratoria. None of these instruments revisits the technology language the MPSC adopted in December 2021; they argue about whether to allow data centers, while accepting the regulatory definition of "clean" power that Bloom helped author.

A future fuel-cell-powered data-center proposal, at a different Michigan site, before a different city council, brought by a different developer, will encounter the same regulatory baseline. Unless the MPSC revises the December 2021 Staff Recommendations or fact-checks the fuel-cell paragraph against independent technical analysis, those decisions will continue to flow through language authored by the company seeking to sell into them.

Lansing residents discovered this because their utility tried to sell them a Bloom Energy data center using the same framings.

What other Michigan jurisdictions are receiving the same framings, from the same company or its data-center customers, today? Who at MPSC drafted the December 2021 fuel-cell paragraph, and against what fact-checking? When the next data-center proposal arrives, in Lansing or another Michigan city, whose definition of "clean" will the public materials use?
